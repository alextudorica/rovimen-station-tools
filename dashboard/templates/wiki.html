Build a meteor camera station

A meteor station is a small camera on your roof and a small computer in your house. This page covers everything you do yourself: getting the camera, choosing the switch and the computer, putting it together, and one command that connects it to ROVIMEN. We set up the rest remotely.

A one-camera station comes to ≈ €300 (1580 RON), a three-camera station to ≈ €500 (2650 RON), both with a used mini-PC. Both are itemised under what it costs.

What a station is

Each camera looks at a fixed patch of the night sky and sends its video down a single network cable, which also powers it. Inside, a small computer records the whole night. In the morning it finds the meteors, sends them to the Global Meteor Network, and puts the clips and night images on this dashboard. When two stations catch the same meteor, the network works out its path through the atmosphere and its orbit.

Nothing on it moves, so once it is set up the station runs on its own for years.

Build the camera yourself

You need the IMX291 camera kit with the 4 mm lens (€55 / 292 RON shipped, checked 26 July 2026), our printed housing and mounting plate, and one cable gland. Tools: a small screwdriver.

  1. Take the IR-cut filter out of the lens holder. It is the small blue-green glass square inside the holder. It blocks infrared light, and taking it out roughly doubles the camera’s sensitivity.
  2. Screw the lens holder onto the board, over the sensor, with its two small screws. Don’t touch the sensor: every mark on it shows in every picture. If dust lands on it, blow it off; never wipe it.
  1. Screw the lens in most of the way. Don’t tighten it: the final focus is done at night, on real stars (see put it together).
  1. Fit the board onto the four posts of the printed mounting plate, lens facing out.
  2. Push the cable through the gland into the housing, and plug its small connectors into the sockets on the back of the board.
  3. Slide the plate into the housing and tighten the gland until the cable no longer moves. The gland is all the sealing it needs.
Test it on the table before it goes up. Plug the camera in once your computer has joined ROVIMEN, and we check the picture and set the camera up remotely. Every problem is ten times easier to fix on the table than on the roof.

Switch and computer

The PoE switch

The switch is the box that powers the cameras through their network cables, so you need no power socket outside. Plug the cameras and the cable from your router into its ports; it sends power only to the devices that need it.

We recommend the TP-Link LS108GP (€43 / 228 RON at eMAG): 8 ports, all with PoE, enough for up to seven cameras plus the router. Any PoE switch works; the one in the photo is a smaller 4-port model.

You also need one outdoor network cable per camera, long enough to reach from the switch to the camera, with the plugs already on. For example this 30 m outdoor Cat6 cable from Nedis (€18 / 94 RON at eMAG).

The computer

A small used office PC is ideal. All night it only records; the heavy work comes in the morning, when it finds the meteors and compresses the night’s video. What to look for:

Processor
Intel Core i5, 8th generation or newer (the model number starts with i5-8…, i5-9… or higher, for example i5-8500T).
Memory
16 GB, as on most of our stations; 8 GB is enough for one or two cameras.
Disk
An SSD. Our stations run one camera on 128 GB, two or three on 256 GB, and five or six on 500 GB. Two cameras also fit on 128 GB if the station keeps less video.
Network
A network socket, connected to your router with a cable.

Good models: HP ProDesk 400 G4 Mini, Lenovo ThinkCentre M720q or M920q, Dell OptiPlex 5060 Micro, HP EliteDesk 800 G4 Mini, Fujitsu Esprimo Q558. Used, they sell for about €130–170 (700–900 RON). This search at Diablos Computer lists used HP office PCs with a suitable i5 and 16 GB; pick a mini or small model.

Already have a Raspberry Pi 5? It can run one camera. We don’t recommend buying one for this: a new Pi 5 costs about as much as a used mini-PC, which does more.

Avoid the very cheap boxes with Celeron, Pentium Silver or Atom processors, and anything with only two cores. They look the same from the outside but cannot keep up with the morning’s work.

Optional: a small UPS (about €57 / 300 RON) where the power often goes off.

Put it together

Cables

  • Router to switch: a normal network cable from the router to the switch’s LAN port.
  • Computer: into the router, or into a free port on the switch.
  • Cameras: one outdoor cable each, into the PoE ports. Leave the addresses to us.

The computer must switch itself back on

Power cuts happen, and nobody is there to press the button. In the computer’s BIOS, find the setting called After Power Loss, Restore on AC Power Loss or AC Recovery, and set it to Power On. You reach the BIOS by pressing a key while the computer starts: F10 on HP, F1 on Lenovo, F2 on Dell and Fujitsu.

Then test it: with the computer running, pull the power plug, wait ten seconds, and plug it back in. It should start on its own. If it doesn’t, the setting did not take.

Mounting and aiming

  • Mount it firmly. The brass thread on top of the housing is the standard 1/4-inch camera thread, so any camera bracket fits, for example these Mozeeda mounts (€7 / 36 RON for two at eMAG). Screw it to a wall or a beam: the camera must not move in the wind, or its measurements go wrong.
  • Aim it 35–45° above the horizon, away from streetlights and your neighbours’ lamps.
  • Nothing in view but sky: no roof edge, no branches.
  • Let the cable hang down from the gland in a small loop before it goes anywhere, so rain drips off instead of running along it.
  • Several cameras? Point them in different directions. We help you choose, so they also overlap with the nearest stations.

Focus

Focus is done at night, on real stars: without the IR-cut filter, a lens focused in daylight is out of focus at night. On the first clear night we open the camera’s live view and tell you which way to turn the lens until the stars are sharp points. It takes a few minutes and one person at the camera.

Install the computer

Install Ubuntu 24.04 LTS Desktop, a free operating system. Ubuntu’s own step-by-step guide shows how to make the USB stick and run the installer.

  • Installing erases the computer’s disk. Use a computer meant only for the station.
  • Choose a user name and a password and keep the password: you need it once, for the join script.
  • Connect it to the router with a cable and leave it on.

If you use a Raspberry Pi you already have, install Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit) on it with Raspberry Pi Imager.

Join ROVIMEN

  1. Ask us for your join script through the panel. Tell us your town, how many cameras you have, and your name.
  2. We send you a small file, named something like rovimen-onboard-yourname.sh. Save it on the station computer.
  3. Open a Terminal in that folder: open the folder where you saved the file, right-click an empty spot, and choose Open in Terminal. Type:
    sudo bash rovimen-onboard-yourname.sh
    with your file’s real name, then your password. Nothing shows on screen while you type the password; that is normal.
  4. Wait a few minutes. It finishes with a line that starts with Done. Tailscale:. Send us that line.

What the script does

  • Connects the computer to the ROVIMEN private network (Tailscale), so we can reach it without opening anything on your router.
  • Lets us log in to install and maintain the station software. Every login is recorded in the computer’s system log.
  • Sets the clock to UTC, which the whole network uses, stops the computer from going to sleep, and stops system updates from restarting it on their own.

What happens next

We install the meteor software, set up your cameras, register them with the Global Meteor Network, and calibrate each one against the stars on the first clear night. You get a login for this dashboard, where your meteors, clips and night images appear every morning.

From then on, just keep it plugged in. The station starts at dusk, works through the morning and early afternoon, uploads, and does it all again the next night. It restarts itself once a day in the afternoon; that is normal.

FAQ

Do I need to know Linux?

No. You install Ubuntu like any other operating system and type one command. We do everything else.

What happens when it’s cloudy?

Nothing bad. The station records, finds nothing, and carries on. Cloudy nights are useful data too.

How much electricity does it use?

A mini-PC with three cameras uses under 60 W, about 1.4 kWh a day.

Does it need a fast internet connection?

No. Any home connection works; the station uploads its results once a day. The full video stays on the station.

What can you do on my computer?

The join script lets us log in to install and maintain the station software, over the ROVIMEN private network. Every login is recorded in the computer’s system log. Use a computer meant only for the station.

Can I see my own meteors?

Yes. You get a login for this dashboard with your station’s clips, night images and timelapses, and your data is part of the Global Meteor Network.

How long does the hardware last?

Years. The parts that eventually wear out are the outdoor cable ends and the video disk, which is written to every night.
